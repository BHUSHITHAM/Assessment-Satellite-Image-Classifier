Part 3:

1. Your classifier turns out to be wrong about 30% of the time. What do you do — and how would you
even decide whether it’s “good enough” to be useful at all? 
I wouldn't call 70% accuracy "good enough" on its own — the real question is where the errors are and whether the confidence score is trustworthy. We saw this directly: our CNN was 81% overall but only 40% accurate on Highway specifically, while other classes were near-perfect, so the fix is per-class, not a blanket retrain. If low-confidence predictions reliably correlate with wrong ones, the model can still be useful even at 70%, as long as those flagged cases go to a human instead of being trusted blindly. From there, I'd have an analyst correct the flagged/wrong predictions and retrain on those corrections (active learning) rather than relabeling everything — though for physically similar classes like Highway and River at this resolution, I wouldn't expect to ever hit 100%, and I'd say so rather than overpromise.

2. This service runs offline, with no one watching it live. A month after deployment, how would you 
know it’s still working correctly? 
Since nothing alerts me live and there's no new ground truth coming in from the field, I can't check "is it still right" directly — I'd instead watch for indirect signs of drift: if average confidence quietly drops over time, or the proportion of low-confidence (flagged) predictions spikes, or the mix of predicted labels suddenly shifts (e.g. everything starts coming back "Highway"), that's a sign something's off, either in the data or the model. On top of that, I'd bundle a small fixed set of tiles with known correct answers and periodically re-run them through the service — if one of those stops classifying correctly, I know something broke (corrupted checkpoint, environment change, code bug) without needing any new labeled data at all.

3. Tiles are coming in fine, but the stored results look wrong. Walk us through how you’d find the 
cause — your actual steps, in order. 
Say tiles started getting classified as "SeaLake" way more often than makes sense, with no errors in the logs. I'd first reproduce it in isolation — run that exact tile through the model directly, outside the API, to confirm the bug is in the model/pipeline and not in serving. Next I'd check what actually got fed to the model (was it resized/normalized correctly, right color channels), then check the class-index mapping specifically, since a class-to-index mismatch between training and serving silently shifts every prediction with no crash at all — this is why we save that mapping inside the checkpoint itself rather than assuming an order. Then I'd confirm which model version actually produced the bad results, check whether the input tiles have genuinely drifted from training data, and only then look at the storage layer (tile ID collisions) last, since it's the least likely cause — cheapest and most probable causes first, storage last.

4. What’s the weakest part of your design, and what would break it first?
Honestly, the model itself — it was trained on only 150 images per class, so it's likely to degrade on genuinely novel imagery (different season, sensor, geography) beyond what we've tested. And tying back to Q2, that degradation would happen silently — no crash, no alert — since there's no live monitoring in production. So the weakest link isn't one thing breaking loudly, it's the combination of a data-limited model and zero visibility into its real-world performance once deployed.
